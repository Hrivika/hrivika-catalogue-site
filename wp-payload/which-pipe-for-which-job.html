Which pipe for which job? uPVC, CPVC, SWR and HDPE explained

Most pipe failures are not caused by bad pipe. They are caused by the right pipe used in the wrong place.

2 Aug 2026 6 min read Hrivika export desk

A hot water line run in uPVC will soften. A drainage stack built with thin-wall pipe will crack. A buried main sized without a surge margin will split at the first pump shutdown. Each of these is a specification mistake, not a manufacturing one.

There are four plastic pipes you will use most often. Each one does a different job. Here is how to tell them apart.

The short answer

  • uPVC — cold water under pressure. Also irrigation, borewell casing and cable duct. Rigid, cheap, glued together with solvent cement.
  • CPVC — hot and cold water. It is the only one of the four you can use for a geyser line.
  • SWR — building drainage and venting. It carries waste by gravity, not under pressure.
  • HDPE — buried mains and irrigation. Flexible, comes in coils, welded into a leak-free line.

Start with temperature

Temperature rules out options faster than anything else.

uPVC starts to soften at about 60°C. That makes it a cold-water material. Use it for cold supply, drainage and irrigation.

CPVC is the same plastic with extra chlorine added. That takes it to about 93°C. This is why CPVC goes on geyser lines, solar hot water and any indoor hot-and-cold plumbing.

There is a catch worth knowing. CPVC loses pressure capacity as it gets hotter. A CTS SDR 11 pipe handles around 27.5 bar at 23°C, but only around 6.9 bar at 82°C. Always check the rating at your actual working temperature, not the number on the front of the brochure.

HDPE for drinking water is rated for roughly 0–45°C, and it also de-rates as temperature climbs.

One warning for hot climates

Every pressure rating is quoted at a reference temperature — 20°C under ISO, 27°C under Indian Standards. A pipe clipped to a rooftop in a Gulf summer sits far above that. Size it with headroom, shade it, or use HDPE.

Then check the pressure rating

You will see two systems on the pipe.

PN is the working pressure in bar. PN 10 means 10 bar. You will see this on metric uPVC and on HDPE.

Schedule is the American system — Schedule 40, 80, 120. For any given size, the outside diameter stays the same across all schedules. Only the wall gets thicker. That is why the same fitting works across all of them.

You will also see SDR on HDPE. It is the outside diameter divided by the wall thickness. A lower SDR means a thicker wall and more pressure. For PE100 water pipe: SDR 11 is roughly PN 16, SDR 17 is roughly PN 10, SDR 26 is roughly PN 6.

On a pumped line, go one SDR thicker than the calculation says. Pumps create surge when they shut down, and surge is what splits pipe.

The class number is a pressure code, not a size. Class 5 is the 10-bar pipe. Sizes usually run from 20 mm to 315 mm, in 6 metre lengths.

SWR is not a pressure pipe

SWR stands for Soil, Waste and Rainwater. It is uPVC, but made to IS 13592, with fittings to IS 14735. It moves waste downhill by gravity and vents foul gas. It holds no pressure at all.

It comes in two wall grades:

  • Type A — thinner. For vent pipes and rainwater downtakes.
  • Type B — thicker. For soil and waste stacks.

Common sizes are 75, 90, 110 and 160 mm.

Do not swap Type B for Type A to save money. The wall thickness is the specification. On vertical stacks, use ring-fit joints rather than glued ones, so the pipe can expand and contract without stressing the joint.

HDPE, and when it beats PVC

HDPE wins where rigid pipe struggles. Long buried mains. Uneven ground. Irrigation across a field. Directional drilling under a road.

It comes in coils up to around 110 mm, and it is joined by heat fusion rather than glue. That makes the joint as strong as the pipe itself, which matters under a road where you cannot dig again.

The trade-offs are real: it costs more, it needs fusion equipment and a trained crew, and it will not bend as tightly as you might hope.

Four mistakes to avoid

  1. uPVC on a hot line. It will soften. Use CPVC.
  2. PVC cement on CPVC. The two cements are different. CPVC needs its own cement and primer.
  3. Ignoring heat on exposed runs. A rooftop line in the Gulf is not working at 20°C.
  4. Mixing sizing systems. Metric and inch pipe will not seat in each other's fittings — the PVC pipe size chart sets out what each system calls the same size.

What to take away

  • Temperature decides first: uPVC and SWR to about 60°C, CPVC to about 93°C, HDPE to about 45°C
  • Only CPVC belongs on a hot water line, and it needs its own cement
  • SWR carries no pressure — Type A for vents, Type B for soil and waste stacks
  • On a pumped line, buy one SDR thicker than the calculation says

Common questions

Can uPVC carry hot water?

No. It softens around 60°C. Use CPVC.

What is the difference between SWR Type A and Type B?

Wall thickness. Type A for vents and rainwater, Type B for soil and waste stacks.

Which is better, HDPE or PVC?

Neither. HDPE for buried mains, PVC for cold water and drainage above ground.

What does PN 10 mean?

The pipe handles 10 bar at 20°C.

What is IS 4985?

The Indian Standard for uPVC drinking water pipe, with pressure classes from 0.25 to 1.25 MPa.
