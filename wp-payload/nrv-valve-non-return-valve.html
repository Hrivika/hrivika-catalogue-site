NRV valve: what it is, the types, and which way it goes

An NRV has no handle and nothing to turn, so nobody looks at it again after the plumber leaves. Fit it facing the wrong way and the line is dead. Put the wrong type on a vertical pipe and you may not find out until the tank has emptied back down the line.

23 Nov 2026 10 min read Hrivika export desk

Most valves get noticed because somebody has to open and close them. A non-return valve works alone, inside the pipe, and that is why mistakes with it stay hidden. Three things decide whether it does its job: the direction it faces, whether the pipe is horizontal or vertical, and the type you bought. All three are settled when the order is written.

What is an NRV, and is it different from a check valve?

NRV stands for non-return valve. It lets water pass in one direction and shuts when the water tries to come back. Flow pushes it open. Reverse flow, a spring or the weight of the moving part closes it.

A check valve is the same thing. "Non-return valve" and "NRV" are the usual words on Indian price lists. "Check valve" is what American catalogues say. One steam engineering tutorial we read opens with the words "check valves, or non-return valves", and treats them as one product (the tutorial). If one supplier quotes you for NRVs and another for check valves, they are quoting the same line.

What are the types of NRV?

The types differ in the part that moves, and that part decides where the valve can be fitted.

Swing valves are the common choice on bigger lines. The flap hangs in the flow, so when a pump stops suddenly it can slam onto the seat, and that slam is one source of water hammer. Lift valves have one moving part, and the same tutorial says they are designed only for horizontal pipelines. Spring-loaded valves close just before the water turns round, and the spring lets some of them work in any position. Dual plate valves sit between two flanges, so they take up little room. Ours is listed as the type that minimises water hammer.

A foot valve is a check valve with a strainer on it. It sits under water at the bottom of the suction pipe and holds the water in the pipe when the pump stops, so the pump does not have to be primed again.

Which way does an NRV go?

Look for the arrow cast or moulded on the body. It points the way the water should travel. On a pump line that is away from the pump. On a supply line into a tank it points towards the tank.

Then check the pipe. This is where orders go wrong.

Disc check valves with a spring are the type the tutorial above clears for vertical lines with the flow going down.

A worked example

Take a pump on the ground filling a roof tank through a 1 inch pipe that rises straight up from the pump outlet. A bronze horizontal check valve is out, because its disc needs a level run to fall back onto the seat. The bronze vertical check valve is built for this spot: spring-loaded, for upward-flowing vertical lines only, ½ to 4 inch, with BSP threads. Write "vertical" on the order and you get the right one. Write only "1 inch NRV, bronze" and it is a coin toss.

Where do you fit one?

  • After a pump. On the delivery side, so the water standing in the rising pipe does not run back through the pump when it stops.
  • At the bottom of a suction pipe. That is the foot valve's place.
  • Where water could run back under gravity. A line coming down from a high tank is the usual case.
  • Before equipment that reverse flow can damage. Meters and strainers are the usual ones.
  • On a drain that can back up. That needs a backflow prevention valve, made for 110 to 200 mm underground drainage.

An NRV does not replace a stop valve. It will hold back reverse flow, but you cannot shut it by hand to work on the line, so a ball valve or gate valve still goes next to it.

What is the NRV on a geyser?

Two different valves get called "the geyser NRV", and they are worth telling apart.

The first comes in the box with a storage water heater. It screws onto the cold water inlet, and the manuals call it a multi-function safety valve. Its job is to let out excess pressure. The second is an ordinary non-return valve in the cold supply pipe, fitted by the plumber so heated water cannot travel back into the cold line.

Buyers ask whether the first can be left off, and where the second should go. We read a current storage-heater manual to check (this one). It says two things worth knowing:

  1. The safety valve must never be removed or blocked to stop it dripping.
  2. Water expands as it heats and tries to flow back up the inlet pipe. The NRV in the cold line stops it, so the extra water leaves through the safety valve as drips. To avoid that dripping, the manual recommends fitting the NRV at least 40 feet from the heater inlet. Other models may give a different figure, so read the manual that came with yours.

The mistakes that cost money

Fitting it backwards. Then it blocks the line completely. On a threaded line it can be unscrewed and turned round. On a solvent-cemented uPVC or CPVC line the valve has to be cut out and a new one bought.

Buying by size and material only. "2 inch NRV" does not say swing, lift or spring, or horizontal or vertical.

Going a size up "to be safe". A check valve that is too big for the flow never opens fully. The tutorial describes what follows as chatter: the valve opens, the pressure drops, it shuts, and the cycle repeats. Match the valve to the pipe size, and to the real flow if your engineer has given one.

Putting a swing check hard against a pump. The same plastic swing-check sheet asks for at least ten pipe diameters between the pump and the valve. On a 2 inch line that is about 20 inches, or half a metre, of straight pipe.

Using uPVC on a hot line. A uPVC NRV is for cold water. For hot lines use the CPVC one or a metal one. Our bronze check valves are listed up to 80°C and the forged brass one up to 90°C. Our pipe comparison covers the plastics.

Which material for which line?

  • uPVC lines: uPVC NRV, nine sizes from ½ inch (15 mm) to 4 inch (100 mm), in schedule 80.
  • CPVC lines: CPVC NRV, five sizes from ¾ inch (20 mm) to 2 inch (50 mm).
  • GI or mixed lines up to 4 inch: bronze or forged brass with BSP threads. If the pipe on either side is plastic, add a threaded adapter each side. Our note on MTA and FTA adapters covers which one.
  • Pump houses and waterworks from 2 inch up: cast iron, either a flanged swing NRV or a dual plate valve between flanges.

For customs, check valves of every material sit under HS 8481.30. The national lines are in our HS code guide.

Common questions

What is the full form of NRV?

Non-return valve. It lets water flow one way and closes by itself when the flow tries to reverse.

Is a check valve the same as a non-return valve?

Yes. They are two names for one product, and "one-way valve" is a third. Indian price lists mostly say NRV, American ones say check valve.

What are the three types of check valves?

The three you meet most in plumbing are swing, lift and spring-loaded. Dual plate valves and foot valves are versions made for flanged lines and for pump suction pipes.

Can an NRV be installed vertically?

Some can. A spring-loaded vertical check or a swing check works with the water going up. A horizontal lift check does not, so say "vertical" when you order.

Should a geyser have a non-return valve?

A storage heater comes with a multi-function safety valve for the cold inlet, and that must never be removed or blocked. A separate NRV in the cold line stops hot water travelling back, but put it at the distance the heater's manual gives, or the safety valve will drip.

What is the difference between a foot valve and an NRV?

A foot valve is an NRV with a strainer, made for the bottom of a pump's suction pipe. An ordinary NRV goes in the line itself, usually after the pump.
